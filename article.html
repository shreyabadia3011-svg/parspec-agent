The first thing you need to understand about construction procurement is how manual it still is.

A contractor sends a 200-page specification document to a distributor. A salesperson opens it, reads through it manually, identifies the products being specified: 480V panels, 3-ton split systems, addressable smoke detectors. The salesperson cross-references them against a product catalogue, prices each one, and assembles a quote. This process takes days. Sometimes a week. For a large MEP project with 500 line items, it can take longer.

Parspec is the company attacking this problem. They use AI to read the spec, match products, and generate quotes automatically. It is the right architectural bet for a fragmented, paper-heavy industry that is among the least digitized in the world.

When I was preparing for my interview at Parspec, I did what I always do when I want to understand a product deeply: I built it.

What I built, and what this article documents, is a working implementation of Parspec's core procurement pipeline, extended with 8 features Parspec currently offers, 4 white-space opportunities in their Submittals & O&M product that no platform is solving today, and a 140-test suite that validates every feature across 12 document variations. All of it runs locally. None of it is a mockup.

Why I built this

I have spent 4.5 years building AI products at Pixis.ai (Prism, an LLM intelligence platform, and Autopilot, an ML campaign engine), Curl Analytics (SARA, a document extraction ETL engine), Mihup, and Capillary. The Parspec problem is structurally identical to problems I have solved before. Building the prototype was how I proved that to myself, and to them.

01

The Problem Parspec Is Solving

To understand what the AI is doing, you need to understand what it is replacing.

In MEP (Mechanical, Electrical, Plumbing) construction, a project specification document describes every product that needs to be installed. It might say: "200A Main Electrical Panel, 40-circuit, NEMA 3R enclosure, Square D or approved equal, Qty 2." That single line requires a distributor to find a matching product in their catalogue, verify it meets every specification parameter, price it, and track it through the entire procurement workflow.

On a hospital project, there are hundreds of such line items. Each one involves: reading the spec, searching the catalogue, verifying compliance, pricing, and then packaging it into a submittal for engineer approval. The process is serial, manual, and error-prone at every step.

What makes this problem particularly interesting from a product perspective is that the variance is not the exception, it is the norm. Spec documents arrive as scanned PDFs, native PDFs, Excel schedules, Word tables, and plain text. Some have explicit "Qty:" labels. Some have quantities in prose ("furnish and install 180 fixtures"). Some use abbreviations ("2x4 LED Troffer 4kK 50W"). An AI system that only handles the clean case is not a production system.

"An AI system that only handles the clean case is not a production system. It is a demo."

This is the core engineering insight that shaped how I built the prototype: the extraction layer has to handle 12 document variations, not just one. The test suite validates exactly this.

The core pipeline processes any construction spec document through four sequential layers. Each layer has a specific job, produces a structured output, and passes it to the next.

Layer 1: Document Inspection

Before extracting anything, the system needs to understand what it is looking at. The inspection layer classifies the document into MEP categories (Electrical, Plumbing, HVAC, Fire Protection, Low Voltage), scores its structure on a 0–100 scale, and makes a routing decision: structured documents with score ≥70 go to the rule-based extraction engine; unstructured documents go to the LLM extraction engine.

This routing decision is the most important architectural choice in the system. Running every document through the LLM is expensive and slow. Running structured documents through regex is fast and deterministic. The hybrid approach pays for LLM inference only when it is actually needed. This is exactly the same principle I applied when building SARA at Curl Analytics, where 70% of documents were structured enough for regex extraction and only 30% required LLM handling.

Layer 2: Requirement Extraction

This is where the real engineering work lives. The extraction layer pulls every product requirement, description, quantity, unit, and confidence score, from the document. Every extracted value is anchored to a verbatim quote from the source document for grounding verification. If the system cannot cite the exact text it extracted a value from, that field is flagged as ungrounded and routed to human review rather than silently passed through.

The extraction layer handles twelve document format variations: clean bullet lists, dense schedule tables, narrative prose, minimal shorthand, typos and abbreviations, and combinations of all of the above. This required twelve regex patterns covering formats from - Product: Qty 10 to Product x 24 to furnish and install 180 fixtures.

Every extracted item gets a confidence score from 0 to 1. Items scoring ≥0.90 are auto-accepted. Items between 0.70–0.89 are flagged for human review. Items below 0.70 are rejected. This three-tier routing is the same pattern I used in Prism's tool-call confidence framework: you only route to human review when the system's uncertainty justifies the cost of human judgment.

Layer 3: Catalogue Matching

Extracted requirements are matched against the 50-product MEP catalogue using a multi-signal fuzzy matching algorithm. A single string similarity ratio is not enough. "LED Troffer" and "2x4 LED Fixture" mean the same thing but share few characters. The matching algorithm combines four signals:

  • Fuzzy string ratio (difflib SequenceMatcher, 40% weight)
  • Category keyword match: electrical keywords against electrical products (20% weight)
  • Spec value hits: voltage, amperage, tonnage matching against product spec fields (8 points per match)
  • Word overlap between description and product name (5 points per shared word)

For every matched item, the system returns the best match, a confidence score, and two ranked alternatives. This is important for the Substitution Intelligence feature: when the first match is out of stock, the alternatives are already pre-scored and ready to present.

Layer 4: Quote Generation

The quote layer assembles matched products into a structured quote grouped by MEP category, applies the distributor's markup percentage, calculates tax, and flags items below confidence thresholds for estimator review before the quote goes out. The output includes a full financial breakdown: subtotal, markup amount, tax, and grand total, all with line-item traceability to the original spec.

Test results: core pipeline

The full pipeline was tested end-to-end on 5 specs including a 50-item convention center MEP spec. The largest spec extracted 48 line items, matched all 48 at 100% match rate, and generated a $847,916 quote in 210ms. The typo-heavy spec still extracted 4 items and matched all 4 correctly.

03

The Pain Points I Was Actually Trying to Solve

Before I describe what I built, I want to be honest about why I built it. The feature list did not come from a product requirements document. It came from sitting with the workflow and asking: where does a distributor's day actually break down?

I mapped the distributor's job end to end. What I found was not one problem but seven distinct failure modes, each one costing real money in a different part of the cycle. The eight features I built are direct responses to those seven failure modes. Nothing more, nothing less.

Pain Point 1: Stock-Outs Kill Quotes at the Finish Line

A distributor spends 45 minutes building a quote. Every product is priced, every line item verified. Then the sales rep checks inventory and finds that the 200A main panel, the anchor item on a $180,000 electrical job, is out of stock for eight weeks. The quote cannot go out. The RFQ deadline is tomorrow.

Today, the rep manually searches their memory and their supplier contacts for an equivalent product. They might find one in 20 minutes. They might spend two hours and come up empty. Either way, the process is entirely unstructured: no scoring, no confidence, no historical signal about whether this substitute has ever been accepted by an engineer on a similar project.

Industry estimates put quote abandonment due to stock-outs at 15–20% of total RFQ volume. At a mid-size distributor processing $10M annually, that is $1.5–2M in quotes that simply never get submitted. The revenue is not lost to a competitor. It disappears entirely because nobody had a fast path from "out of stock" to "here is an equivalent."

What I built in response

Substitution Intelligence: the moment a product is flagged OOS or discontinued, the system immediately surfaces ranked alternatives scored on three signals: spec match (how technically equivalent is it), price delta (how much does it change the quote), and historical acceptance rate (has this substitute been approved before on similar projects). The estimator makes a one-click decision instead of a two-hour manual search.

Pain Point 2: Distributors Are Guessing on Pricing

Ask any distributor sales rep how they set markup on a competitive RFQ. The honest answer is: experience and gut feel. They know roughly what wins on office jobs and roughly what loses on hospital jobs. But they have no systematic view of their own win rate by markup tier, no signal on how fast they are relative to competitors, and no data on how their quote value compares to the market.

The result is predictable: they leave margin on the table on sole-source projects where they could price higher, and they over-price on competitive bids where speed and margin are both working against them. A distributor with 200 active quotes might be making this pricing mistake dozens of times per week without ever knowing it.

The financial impact is asymmetric. Winning a quote at 2% lower margin than necessary costs money. Losing a quote because the margin was 3% too high costs the entire revenue. The error in one direction is quantifiable only in retrospect. The error in the other direction is invisible: you simply never hear about the RFQs you did not win.

What I built in response

Two features, working together. Win Probability gives the estimator a signal before they submit: this quote at 22% markup on an office project has a 74% win probability; dropping to 19% raises it to 85%. Dynamic Pricing goes further, optimizing the markup recommendation based on customer tier, project size, urgency, and project type before the estimator has to decide. The combination replaces gut feel with a data-driven recommendation that gets better with every quote outcome captured.

Pain Point 3: Large Distributors Are Drowning in RFQ Volume

A distributor receiving 40 RFQs on a Monday morning cannot give each one equal attention. They triage manually: scan email subjects, remember which customers are important, guess at which projects are worth the quoting time. High-value opportunities that arrive from unfamiliar contractors get deprioritized. Low-value RFQs from familiar customers get worked first. The triage logic is entirely relationship-based, not value-based.

The downstream consequence is systematic: the distributor's team is optimizing for familiarity rather than revenue. They are working the comfortable queue, not the valuable one. By the time they get to the $400,000 convention center MEP job from a new contractor, the deadline has passed.

What I built in response

Batch Processing with auto-triage: the entire morning's inbox is processed in parallel, with results ranked by estimated quote value and win probability. The estimator opens their dashboard and sees: Hotel renovation, $196,967, 78% win probability, quote today. Not because someone told them to, but because the system sorted the queue by opportunity value automatically.

Pain Point 4: Submitted Prices Don't Match Actual Costs at Order Time

A distributor quotes a 5-ton Trane rooftop unit at $6,400 based on their catalogue. The quote wins. Three weeks later, when they go to place the order, the manufacturer's current price is $6,820. The distributor absorbs a $420 per-unit margin hit on a job with 12 units. That is $5,040 in unexpected cost and an awkward conversation with the customer about repricing.

This happens because catalogues are static. Prices change. Lead times change. Products get discontinued or go on allocation. The catalogue in the quoting system reflects the world as it was when it was last updated, not the world as it is today. For fast-moving product categories in a supply-constrained market, this gap can be significant.

What I built in response

Manufacturer Live Pricing: before a quote goes out, the system pulls real-time price, stock status, and lead time directly from manufacturer ERPs. Items with more than 5% deviation from catalogue price are flagged explicitly. The estimator sees the live price, the catalogue price, and the delta, and decides whether to absorb it, pass it through, or find an alternative before the quote is submitted rather than after it wins.

Pain Point 5: Nobody Knows What the Market Is Specifying

A distributor's view of the market is limited to their own RFQ inbox. They can see what their customers are asking for. They cannot see what is being specified across the industry. If EV charging circuit requirements are doubling across commercial projects in their region, they will not know until those specs start arriving. If a major manufacturer is having lead time problems that are driving engineers to spec alternatives, they find out the hard way.

This information asymmetry is expensive in both directions: distributors are caught off guard by demand shifts they could have anticipated, and they miss stocking opportunities in categories that are growing before their competitors notice.

What I built in response

Spec-to-Market Trends Dashboard: aggregated and anonymized intelligence across all distributor activity. Top specified products by volume, emerging categories with growth drivers (EV charging +210% driven by state code changes), declining categories with reasons (fluorescent fixtures -78% due to LED mandate), and monthly volume trends. This is the feature that is only possible at the network level. No individual distributor can build it alone. Parspec can.

Pain Point 6: Wrong Products Make It Into Quotes Undetected

An estimator matches a 3-ton split AC unit to a California project specification. The product they matched is 14 SEER. The ASHRAE 90.1-2022 minimum for Climate Zone 3B in California is 15 SEER. The quote goes out. The engineer rejects the submittal. Eight days later, after resubmission with the correct product, the job proceeds, but the distributor has lost a week and created a compliance problem that will now be in the engineer's memory the next time they see a submittal from this distributor.

This happens not because estimators are careless but because code compliance is a moving target. ASHRAE 90.1 updates every three years. State energy codes diverge from federal minimums. ADA requirements vary by project type. Keeping a mental model of all applicable codes for every product category in every jurisdiction is not a reasonable expectation of a sales rep whose primary job is to quote, not to engineer.

What I built in response

Spec Compliance Checker: before a quote is submitted, the system cross-checks every matched product against applicable codes for the project type and state. SEER violations, missing NEMA classifications, emergency backup requirements, ADA compliance: all flagged with the specific code reference and a suggested fix. The estimator catches the problem before the engineer does, which is the only version of catching it that does not cost time and trust.

Pain Point 7: Every Quote Outcome Is a Lost Learning Opportunity

When a distributor wins a quote, nobody records why. When they lose one, nobody records why. When an engineer rejects a submittal, the rejection reason lives in a PDF attachment in someone's email. When a product substitution gets accepted by an engineer, that acceptance is never fed back into the system that suggested the substitution.

Every quote outcome is a learning signal. Every approval is a confirmation that the matching logic was right. Every rejection is a correction that should make the next submission better. Every substitution acceptance is a data point about product equivalence that the entire network could benefit from. None of this signal is being captured today. It evaporates into inboxes and chat threads and the heads of experienced reps who eventually retire.

What I built in response

The HITL Review Queue captures rejection reasons as structured training signal, not free text but categorized: "Wrong product / Wrong quantity / Not in catalogue / OCR extraction error / Other." The Win Probability model learns from captured quote outcomes. The Engineer Prediction model learns from submittal approval and rejection history. The Substitution Engine learns from accepted substitute proposals. The entire system gets smarter with every transaction, but only if the learning infrastructure is built in from day one.

"The features are not features. They are seven answers to seven questions that a distributor faces every day and currently has no systematic way to answer."

The Win Probability Feature in Depth

The win probability model is worth explaining in detail because it illustrates a principle that runs through every feature: the goal is not to replace human judgment, it is to give humans better information before they make a judgment.

A distributor receiving 200 RFQs per week cannot give each one equal attention. They are currently making triage decisions by gut feel, which customer do I know, which project seems big, which quote do I feel good about. The win probability model makes that triage data-driven.

The model scores four signals. Markup competitiveness gets the highest weight (35%) because it is the most controllable variable: every percentage point of markup above the historical winning average for that project type reduces win probability by approximately 8 points. Submission speed gets 30%, quotes submitted in under 24 hours win significantly more often than quotes submitted after 3 days. The model surfaces this signal explicitly so estimators understand the cost of delay.

Directional test results

The model correctly ranks: markup 15% scores 85% vs markup 30% scores 66% for the same office project. Submission in 1 day scores 94% vs 5 days scores 70% for the same quote. Both directional relationships pass deterministically, with no randomness in the scoring.

The White Space: Four Things Parspec Isn't Doing Yet

The most interesting PM work is not building what everyone can see, it is finding what nobody has noticed yet. For Parspec's Submittals & O&M product, I identified four white space opportunities that exist in the workflow after quote acceptance and before project closeout. None of them are being solved today.

"Parspec reduces submittal creation from 3 days to 30 minutes. But a single engineer rejection adds 8 days back. The highest-value feature is not faster creation. It is rejection prevention."

White Space 1: Submittal Revision Tracker

Engineers reject submittals constantly. Wrong voltage rating. Missing NEMA classification. SEER rating that does not meet the current energy code. When a rejection comes back, the distributor receives a redlined PDF, manually hunts through it to find what changed, re-pulls the correct product cut sheets, and resubmits. The entire cycle takes 8–14 days on average.

Parspec currently has no revision tracking. The system creates the initial submittal but has no concept of revision cycles, rejection reasons, or cost impact of specification upgrades required by the engineer.

I built a revision tracker that diffs Rev 1 against Rev 2, shows exactly which items were rejected and why, what fix was applied, and what it cost. For the Downtown Medical Center test case: 2 items rejected in Rev 1 (NEMA classification mismatch on the panel, SEER violation on the AC unit), both fixed in Rev 2, 8 days lost, $340 in cost impact from the spec upgrade. This is data that today exists only in someone's email inbox.

White Space 2: Engineer Approval Prediction

Some engineers always reject Eaton panels on hospital projects. Some engineering firms require BACnet-compatible controls on all commercial projects. Some AHJs in specific states enforce energy codes that others routinely accept variances for. This is tribal knowledge that lives in the heads of experienced sales reps, and leaves when they do.

The engineer prediction feature scores each item in a submittal package for approval probability before it goes out, based on the historical rejection patterns and brand preferences of the specific engineering firm. For Jacobs Engineering on a California hospital project: Eaton panel scores 73% (known pattern: 78% rejection rate on hospital projects, prefers Square D). Switching the brand to Square D raises the package score to 81%. The decision is made before the engineer ever sees the submittal.

This feature is also the data moat made actionable. Every submittal Parspec processes, every approval and rejection, every brand preference that emerges, all of it becomes training signal for the prediction model. After 300 distributors and thousands of projects, Parspec's approval prediction capability becomes essentially impossible to replicate from scratch.

White Space 3: O&M Auto-Assembly

When a construction project closes, the building owner receives an Operations & Maintenance manual containing warranty terms, maintenance schedules, spare parts lists, and certification documentation for every installed product. This package is assembled manually, often weeks after project completion, by going back to every manufacturer to re-download documents that were already available at submittal time.

The O&M assembly feature builds this package automatically at submittal creation time, at zero additional cost because the data is already present. For a 10-product installation, the system assembles: 596 pages of combined documentation, warranty terms for every product (ranging from 1 year to 20 years for Lennox heat exchangers), structured maintenance schedules, and spare parts lists, in seconds rather than hours.

Time savings demonstrated

3 products: 2.2 hours saved. 10 products: 7.5 hours saved. 20+ product full MEP: estimated 15+ hours saved at project closeout. The time saving compounds: it is not just the assembly time, it is the re-assembly time when products get substituted during construction and the O&M package needs updating.

White Space 4: Multi-Distributor Collaboration

On a large project, a convention center, a hospital, a data center, the MEP scope is split across multiple distributors. One handles electrical. One handles HVAC. One handles fire protection. One handles plumbing. Each creates their submittal independently. Nobody is coordinating across scopes.

The result: voltage mismatches that only surface at installation. Duplicate scope where two distributors quote the same disconnects. Discontinued products that block O&M package assembly at closeout. These conflicts are currently discovered by general contractors in an email thread, usually at the worst possible time.

I built a shared project workspace with AI conflict detection across distributor scopes. For the Metro Convention Center test case, $4.2M project, 4 distributors, the system detected 3 conflicts automatically:

The Test Suite: 140 Tests, 12 Document Variations

Building a prototype without testing it is not a prototype, it is a demo. I wrote a 140-test suite that covers every feature, every edge case, and every document format variation the system claims to handle.

The test suite revealed two real bugs that would have embarrassed me in a demo:

  • Empty document false positive: The inspection layer was detecting "HVAC" from the word "no" appearing adjacent to "cooling" in the phrase "no cooling systems." Fixed with a word-count guard: documents under 10 words return empty categories.
  • Extraction only handling Qty: format: The original regex patterns only matched - Product: Qty 10. Table-style specs (Product x 24), prose specs (furnish and install 180 fixtures), and shorthand specs (2 units) all failed. Fixed by adding 7 new patterns covering all major format variations.

The directional tests are worth calling out specifically. It is not enough for a model to return a number, the number has to behave correctly in response to inputs. A win probability model that scores a 30% markup higher than a 15% markup on the same project is broken, even if both numbers are in range. The test suite validates directionality explicitly for every model feature.

07

The Data Moat Argument

Most people think about Parspec as a workflow automation tool. Faster quotes. Faster submittals. Fewer manual hours. That is the immediate value proposition and it is real.

But the strategic value is something different, and understanding it is the difference between a feature-level PM and a platform-level PM.

Every RFQ Parspec processes adds a data point. Which products were specified. Which products were matched. Which substitutions were proposed. Which ones the engineer approved. Which quotes won. Which ones lost and at what markup. Over 300 distributors and thousands of projects, Parspec is accumulating something no individual distributor can ever have: a cross-industry view of the entire MEP procurement cycle.

That data becomes three moats simultaneously:

  • The substitution moat: Parspec knows that Eaton BR200 ≈ Square D QO142L200PG because it has seen that substitution accepted hundreds of times across dozens of engineer firms. A competitor starting from scratch cannot buy this knowledge.
  • The prediction moat: Parspec knows that Jacobs Engineering rejects Eaton panels on hospital projects 78% of the time. This pattern is invisible to any single distributor. It is only visible at the network level.
  • The pricing moat: Parspec knows the actual clearing price for 200A panels in Chicago in Q3 better than any individual distributor does. Anonymized and aggregated, this becomes pricing intelligence that is genuinely impossible to reverse-engineer.

"After five years of data across 300 distributors, Parspec's knowledge graph of product equivalences, engineer preferences, and market pricing becomes the single most valuable asset in construction procurement. That is not a workflow tool. That is infrastructure."

The features I built are proof-of-concept implementations of how that data moat becomes product value. The substitution engine, the engineer prediction model, the win probability scorer, the market trends dashboard, each one is a different expression of the same underlying insight: Parspec's real product is not the UI. It is the accumulated intelligence of every transaction that has ever run through the platform.

08

What I Would Build Next

If I were sitting in the PM seat at Parspec tomorrow, my 90-day roadmap would have three priorities.

First: Ship the Substitution Engine and Engineer Prediction as a bundled "First-Time-Right" feature. The framing matters: these are not two separate features, they are one capability: submittals that get approved the first time. Measure success by revision cycle reduction, not by feature adoption. A distributor whose average revision cycles drop from 1.8 to 1.1 knows exactly what that improvement is worth.

Second: Instrument every quote outcome. Win, loss, revision cycle count, days-to-quote, final markup. This data is the training signal for every prediction model. Without it, the intelligence layer has nothing to learn from. Building the data collection infrastructure is more valuable in Q1 than shipping another UI feature.

Third: Build the engineer portal, a lightweight preference-setting interface for engineer firms. When engineers broadcast their spec preferences (preferred manufacturers, required certifications, annotation format) directly to the platform, every distributor's first-pass approval rate improves simultaneously. This is the network effect that turns Parspec from a distributor tool into construction procurement infrastructure.

What Building This Taught Me

I have built three AI products from scratch, Prism (LLM marketing intelligence), SARA (document extraction ETL), and Autopilot (ML campaign optimization). Each time, the hardest part was not the model. The hardest part was the interface between the model and reality.

For Parspec, that interface is the extraction layer. Construction spec documents are not like financial reports or customer emails. They have been designed over decades for humans to read, not machines to parse. They use abbreviations that are obvious to a licensed PE and opaque to an algorithm. They mix structured data (quantity schedules) with unstructured data (narrative specifications) in ways that vary by engineering firm, project type, region, and era.

The lesson from SARA, which I brought directly into this prototype, is that you do not solve document variance with a better model. You solve it with a routing architecture that sends each document to the extraction method it is most suited for. The model is the fallback for the cases the rules cannot handle, not the primary extraction path.

The second lesson is about confidence scores. Every AI output in an enterprise context needs a confidence score, and the confidence score needs to be calibrated to a real-world threshold. A confidence score that routes everything above 50% to auto-accept is not a confidence score, it is a false sense of safety. The thresholds I set in this prototype (≥0.90 auto-accept, 0.70–0.89 review, <0.70 reject) come from the same calibration work I did on SARA's extraction engine at Curl Analytics, where the equivalent thresholds were set by measuring the correlation between model confidence and actual extraction accuracy on a holdout set of 500 documents.

The third lesson is the one that applies to every AI product regardless of domain: the question is not whether the AI can do it. The question is how it communicates its uncertainty, and what happens when it is uncertain.

A prototype that silently passes through low-confidence extractions and lets them appear in a customer's quote is not a product. It is a liability. The HITL routing layer, the confidence score display, the verbatim quote grounding, the estimator review queue: these are not bells and whistles. They are the architecture of trust.

Parspec is building something important. The $13 trillion construction industry running on spreadsheets and phone calls is not a niche problem. It is a structural inefficiency that has been waiting for exactly this combination of LLMs, document intelligence, and MEP domain knowledge. Building this prototype was how I proved to myself that I understand the problem deeply enough to help solve it.
